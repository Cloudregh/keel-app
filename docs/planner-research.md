# Seed the priors, then let one user teach the planner

A fully on-device planner can learn task weight, real duration and personal energy with three small Bayesian models totalling a few hundred lines of plain JavaScript, and can build a day plan in a few milliseconds. The design that holds up best has five parts. The first is a **multinomial naive Bayes effort classifier seeded with a heavy/light keyword lexicon as Dirichlet pseudo-counts**. The second is a **conjugate Bayesian regression on log(actual ÷ estimate)** that returns both a likely duration and a "plan-for" P80. The third is an **hour-of-week energy curve estimated as Beta/Gamma posteriors shrunk toward a peak, dip and rebound prior**. The fourth is a **greedy-then-local-search plan builder** that puts demanding work in high-energy hours, batches light tasks into the afternoon trough, inserts breaks, and leaves about a quarter of the day unscheduled. The fifth closes the loop: **Thompson sampling over a few ordering strategies and a pairwise perceptron learned from drags and completion order** tune the plan, and every placement carries a one-line reason and an undo control. The science behind the defaults is uneven. The planning fallacy is strong and replicated. Time-of-day "synchrony" effects are real but appear in only about half of young-adult studies. Popular block lengths such as 52/17 and 90-minute cycles rest on observational or contested evidence. The willpower-depletion rationale for "eat the frog" largely failed replication. So the central design principle is to **treat every constant as a prior that the user's own data overrides**. The biggest practical risks are not model choice but data hygiene: feedback loops when the model's own suggestions become training labels, bad timer data, and drift.

## The evidence supports matching work to hours, not any magic block length

The strongest scheduling lever is putting demanding work at the person's own best time, but the effect is less reliable than popular books suggest. A 2025 systematic review of 65 studies found that **only 45–49% of young-adult studies showed a synchrony effect**, mostly in attention, inhibition and memory. In older adults the figure was **83% (10 of 12 studies)**. More than 80% of studies found no main effect of chronotype: no type is smarter overall, and what matters is matching task to time ([Chauhan et al. 2025](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/31067/6/FullText.pdf)). Morning types reach maximum activation **about three hours earlier** than evening types. Arousal generally rises through the morning, dips in the early afternoon and rebounds in late afternoon ([Wiłkość-Dębczyńska & Liberacka-Dwojak 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10683050)). Large-scale mood data show the same shape. Positive affect peaks in the morning, falls through the workday and recovers later, and **on weekends the morning peak arrives about two hours later** ([Golder & Macy via ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/09/110929144632.htm)). Simple tasks are unlikely to vary by time of day ([Freethink](https://www.freethink.com/health/circadian-rythym-peak)). That is the justification for applying the energy term mostly to heavy tasks. One single-study result found insight problems solved more often at the *non*-optimal time while analytic problems were unaffected ([BPS Research Digest on Wieth & Zacks 2011](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/youre-most-creative-when-youre-your-groggiest)). It deserves at most a low-weight "creative work can go off-peak" option.

Ordering doctrine is weaker still. The ego-depletion idea behind "do hard things before willpower runs out" fell from d = 0.62 in the original meta-analysis to **d = 0.04 in a 23-lab preregistered replication** ([Hagger & Chatzisarantis 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.00621/pdf)). A planner should therefore not model a depleting willpower budget. The better-supported mechanisms are switching costs, which are consistent and grow with rule complexity ([summary of Rubinstein, Meyer & Evans 2001](https://mentalmather-site.pages.dev/articles/context-switching-cognitive-cost)), and attention residue, where an unfinished task lingers and hurts performance on the next one ([Leroy 2009](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jobhdp:v:109:y:2009:i:2:p:168-181)). A third is the progress principle: diary data show visible progress on meaningful work is the strongest positive driver of motivation ([Progress Principle overview](https://concepts.dsebastien.net/concept/progress-principle/)). Together these support a hybrid order. Open with one quick win of 15 minutes or less, put the most important hard task in the first peak block (later for owls), batch light tasks by context, and prefer finishing a task over splitting it. No controlled study compares "hardest first" with "easiest first", so this is a reasoned default rather than a proven one.

Break and block evidence is the weakest of all. The 52-minute work / 17-minute break pattern came from DeskTime's observational data on its most "productive" users, and **the same vendor's 2021 rerun produced 112/26**. The numbers are not stable thresholds ([Silicon Canals critique](https://siliconcanals.com/t-desktime-52-17-work-break-productivity-observational-data/)). The 90-minute basic rest-activity cycle is well documented in sleep, but its waking version has been disputed since 1977 ([Wikipedia: BRAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_rest%E2%80%93activity_cycle)). The best experimental evidence, a meta-analysis of 22 micro-break studies, found that breaks of 10 minutes or less **raise vigor and reduce fatigue but have no significant overall effect on performance**, and that longer breaks help performance more ([Albulescu et al. 2022 via EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/962663)). On daily capacity, Ericsson's review of deliberate practice found "essentially no benefit from durations exceeding 4 hr per day" and reduced benefit beyond 2 hours ([Matuschak's notes on Ericsson 1993](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/z6xWa5QDyUmvuDqZ9Z7sANrfjf8sLoJ7BnspW)). That supports a **4-hour hard cap and roughly 3-hour target for heavy work**. The defensible defaults are a 60-minute focus block (range 25–90, with 25/5 Pomodoro as a user option), a break of about 20% of the block (5–20 minutes), and a 45-minute lunch. All of them should be presented as adjustable.

The planning fallacy is the one finding strong enough to hard-code as a prior. In the classic thesis study, students predicted 33.9 days, gave a worst case of 48.6, and actually took **55.5 days, about 1.64× their prediction and longer than their own worst case**. Only about 30% finished on time. For personal projects, **only 45% finished by their "99% sure" date** ([Wikipedia: Planning fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planning_fallacy); [Buehler et al. 1994](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/buehler-et-al-1994.pdf)). Task-level data from software are consistent with this. In practitioner time-tracking data, actual time given an estimate is roughly **lognormal with log-scale σ ≈ 1**, so the mean is about 1.6× the estimate and the 95th percentile about 5× ([Task estimation: Conquering Hofstadter's Law](https://thesearesystems.substack.com/p/task-estimation-conquering-hofstadters)). In 8,252 small tasks, estimates were off by roughly a factor of 2 within one standard deviation, and the over/under factor depended on estimate size ([shape-of-code 2022](https://shape-of-code.com/2022/06/19/over-under-estimation-factor-for-most-estimates/)). The months-long thesis projects overstate bias for 20-minute errands. A cold-start multiplier of **1.25 for tasks under 30 minutes, rising to 1.5 for tasks of 2 hours or more**, is therefore a reasonable assumption to replace with learned values. The short-task figure is an engineering choice, not a measured one.

## Seeded naive Bayes learns task heaviness from a few dozen examples

With tens to a few hundred labelled to-dos, a generative classifier is the right starting point. Ng and Jordan showed that although logistic regression has lower asymptotic error, a generative classifier "may also approach its (higher) asymptotic error much faster", and that there are two regimes as data grows ([Ng & Jordan 2001](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2001/hash/7b7a53e239400a13bd6be6c91c4f6c4e-Abstract.html)). Multinomial naive Bayes has a closed-form count update. That makes it natively online, lets it accept fractional (soft) labels, allows exact "unlearning" by subtracting an item's old counts when the user relabels it, and makes explanations easy. Rennie et al. identified a "skewed data bias": the class with less data gets systematically lower weights. Their complement-NB fix raised accuracy sharply on small classes, for example **Reuters macro-averaged accuracy from 0.270 to 0.647** ([Rennie et al. 2003](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs276/handouts/rennie.icml03.pdf)). A real to-do list is likely to be mostly Light, so either compute complement weights from the same count table (no extra state needed) or flatten the class prior to π_c^0.5. FTRL-proximal logistic regression, whose per-coordinate update stores only two numbers per feature and yields exact zeros under L1 ([FTRL derivation notes](https://junaidaliop.github.io/zij/math/ftrl/)), is a sound upgrade. It should be added only once there are about 300 labels and naive Bayes accuracy has plateaued, and even then blended with naive Bayes rather than replacing it.

The keyword lexicon fits in as **Dirichlet pseudo-counts kept in a separate read-only table** and added at prediction time. Rennie's estimator already allows a per-feature smoothing term, so a seed word is simply a word with a larger prior count. With seed strength s = 3 for strong words and 1.5 for weak ones, on top of base Laplace smoothing of α = 0.5, about three contradicting real observations neutralize a seed word. Keeping seeds separate lets forgetting decay only learned evidence, never the prior, and lets the lexicon change without retraining. Heavy seeds include verbs such as write, draft, design, build, research, prepare, migrate, refactor, report and proposal. Light seeds include email, reply, call, pay, book, schedule, send, buy and confirm. Medium seeds include review, edit, update, fix, read and organize. Metadata can be seeded too: an estimate of 15 minutes or less suggests Light, 120 minutes or more suggests Heavy, and four or more subtasks suggests Heavy.

Feature engineering matters more than the algorithm for 3–15-token titles. The recommended pipeline is lowercase, numbers and URLs replaced with placeholders, a roughly 40-word stoplist, and light suffix stripping. Features are unigrams plus adjacent bigrams, binarized to presence or absence (Rennie's term-frequency and length transforms are near no-ops on titles), plus an extra `v:` feature for the first token, because the leading verb is the strongest effort signal. Notes get a `n:` prefix and half weight. Metadata is emitted as categorical tokens (priority, project, bucketed estimate, subtask count, due-date bucket), since naive Bayes handles raw numbers poorly. A string-keyed sparse map is better than feature hashing here. Signed hashing is unbiased, but its error comes from collisions that shrink only as the table grows ([Weinberger et al. 2009](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0902.2206v5.pdf)). At the 2^16 buckets needed to keep collisions near 2% for a 1,500-word vocabulary, a hashed table is larger than a pruned sparse map of about 100 KB for 3,000 tokens.

Three refinements turn the raw classifier into something trustworthy. First, naive Bayes sums correlated token evidence and is overconfident. Temperature-scale the log-scores (T ≈ 1 + 0.15 × number of active features, or fit T online on recent labels) and show "unsure" whenever the top probability is below 0.5. Second, weight label sources by how much they reveal. An explicit correction counts 1.0 and replaces any earlier label for that item. An implicit label from tracked time counts 0.5, using thresholds of Light < 30 minutes, Medium 30–120 and Heavy > 120, with soft splits within ±25% of a boundary. A silent acceptance of the suggestion counts at most 0.3, because acceptance is weak evidence and amplifies feedback loops. Third, decay counts by γ = 0.995 per label (an effective memory of about 200 labels). This can be done in O(1) with a global scale factor. Evaluate prequentially, predicting each new label before training on it, rather than holding back data the user has paid for. None of these weights has been validated on personal-planner data. They are starting points to tune against prequential log loss.

## Predicting log(actual ÷ estimate) yields both a likely time and a safe time

Duration errors are multiplicative: a 30-minute task that runs 2× and a 4-hour task that runs 2× are the same mistake. The right target is therefore **y = ln(actual ÷ user estimate)**, or ln(actual minutes) when there is no estimate. In log space, noise is roughly Gaussian, the median correction is exp(mean), and a P80 is a simple shift. The arithmetic mean of raw ratios should never be used, because long-tail overruns bias it upward. Joel Spolsky's Evidence-Based Scheduling is the practitioner precedent. It divides each estimate by a randomly drawn historical velocity across about 100 Monte Carlo scenarios, discards history older than about six months, and gives new estimators a fabricated wide history until about six real tasks are done ([Spolsky 2007](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2007/10/26/evidence-based-scheduling/)). He also notes the key insight that consistent bias is easy to correct while erratic estimates produce wide predictions that should not be trusted.

The compact, principled version is **Normal-Inverse-Gamma Bayesian linear regression with a forgetting factor**. Applying forgetting leaves the posterior mean unchanged and widens the uncertainty ([bayesianbandits NIG docs](https://bayesianbandits.readthedocs.io/en/latest/math/normal-inverse-gamma.html)). It updates one observation at a time with a recursive-least-squares (Sherman–Morrison) step at O(p²) cost, which means fewer than 200 numbers for 6–14 features. The feature vector is [1, ln(estimate/30), isMedium, isHeavy, ln(1+subtasks), isHighPriority, and indicators for the top eight projects]. The ln(estimate) slope captures the finding that the over/under factor depends on estimate size ([shape-of-code](https://shape-of-code.com/2022/06/19/over-under-estimation-factor-for-most-estimates/)). Project effects get tight zero-mean priors, which is equivalent to partial pooling. In that approach a group's estimate is a precision-weighted average of its own mean and the global mean, so sparse projects are pulled toward the overall pattern ([Fonnesbeck multilevel modeling](https://nbviewer.org/github/fonnesbeck/multilevel_modeling/blob/master/multilevel_modeling.ipynb)). The simple closed form is m_g = (n_g·ȳ_g + k·ȳ_global)/(n_g + k) with k ≈ 4. The posterior predictive is a Student-t in log space, so **median = estimate × e^m** and **P80 = estimate × e^(m + t₀.₈·scale)**. The t-quantile can be approximated without libraries as z + (z³+z)/(4ν) with z = 0.8416. Under the σ = 1 lognormal model, P80 works out to about 2.3× the median, which explains why a single "padded" number feels either too long or too short.

| Duration-model default | Value | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Intercept prior | mean ln 1.25 ≈ 0.22, sd 0.5 | Mild planning-fallacy prior; learned per user |
| Slope and project priors | mean 0, sd 0.3 (effort class 0.4) | Shrinkage toward the global pattern |
| Noise prior a₀, b₀ | 3, 1.0 | Implies σ ≈ 0.7 in log space |
| Forgetting γ | 0.98 per completed task | About 50-task memory; count-based so vacations don't erase it |
| Winsorize y | [−1.6, +1.6] (0.2×–5×) | A forgotten overnight timer would otherwise swamp the model |
| Ignore actuals | < 1 min or > 8 h | Checked off without work, or a timer left running |
| No-estimate prior | Light 15, Medium 45, Heavy 120 min, sd 0.7 | Separate model on ln(actual minutes) |
| Show P80 | after about 15 completed tasks | Before that, the P80 mostly reflects the prior |

Two implementation details prevent subtle failures. First, record each estimate's provenance and **train the bias model only on user-authored estimates**. If the user accepts the model's suggested estimate, actual ÷ suggestion measures the model, not the user, and feeding it back creates a self-confirming loop. Second, guard the recursive update against "covariance windup" under forgetting by symmetrizing the covariance matrix, capping its diagonal at ten times the prior variance, and resetting it on NaN. For the day's total, summing per-task medians understates the total because lognormals are right-skewed. Run a Monte Carlo of about 200 draws per task, as in Evidence-Based Scheduling, which is trivial in the browser.

## The energy curve is a smoothed posterior over 168 hours, not a chronotype label

No peer-reviewed source gives an hour-by-hour population focus curve for knowledge work, so the cold-start curve has to be built by hand to match the documented shape. That shape is a morning ramp, a late-morning peak, an early-afternoon dip, a smaller late-afternoon rebound, and the same curve shifted about two hours later on weekends ([Golder & Macy via ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/09/110929144632.htm); [PMC10683050](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10683050)). A workable weekday prior for someone who wakes at 07:00, on a 0–1 scale, is 0.35 at 07:00, 0.55 at 08:00, 0.75 at 09:00, **0.85 at 10:00–11:00**, 0.70 at noon, 0.55 at 13:00, **0.50 at 14:00**, 0.55 at 15:00, 0.65 at 16:00, **0.70 at 17:00**, then declining to 0.60, 0.50, 0.45 and 0.40 through 21:00, with night hours near 0.1. When the user's typical first-activity hour is known, shift the curve by the difference from 07:00. Rise Science similarly anchors its energy timeline to the user's sleep and wake timing. It describes a post-wake "grogginess zone" in which sleep inertia typically lasts 60–90 minutes, and it recommends simple tasks then and challenging work at the first peak ([Rise Science](https://risescience.com/blog/how-to-have-more-energy-in-the-morning)). An onboarding question about chronotype can shift the prior by about −1.5 hours for larks and +2 hours for owls, consistent with the three-hour lark–owl gap. Treat this as a nudge to the prior, not a fixed classification, given how inconsistent synchrony effects are in young adults.

Personalization comes from treating each hour-of-week as a small conjugate estimate. Share signals, such as focus minutes or productive-app time over active time, update a **Beta posterior**. Scale minutes down (about 10 active minutes per pseudo-trial) so one hour cannot overwhelm the prior. Count signals, such as task completions or app switches per active hour, update a **Gamma-Poisson posterior** with exposure as the rate. Exposure is the crucial detail. An hour the user is never active, such as 03:00, carries no evidence and stays at the prior, and raw ratios over zero exposure must never be computed. Fuse the signals as a convex combination. A reasonable unvalidated starting point is 0.4 focus share, 0.3 productive share, 0.2 relative completion rate and 0.1 inverted switch rate. Rize's practice of counting "deep work" as sessions with no app-switching or idle gaps shows these signals are what commercial trackers actually use ([Rize](https://rize.io/features/productivity)). Each day, decay all pseudo-counts toward the prior with λ = 0.97 (about a 33-day memory). Keep separate weekday and weekend curves, and blend the weekend curve with the shifted weekday curve (κ ≈ 3 days) until about four weekend days exist. For a planner without passive tracking, the minimal signals are completion of heavy tasks by start hour plus an optional one-tap energy rating. Both update the same Beta/Gamma tables.

Smooth with a circular Gaussian kernel (σ ≈ 1 hour, wrapping at midnight), then rescale each user's curve to [0, 1] so it expresses relative "best hours" rather than absolute energy. Cosinor regression, the standard circadian tool, is tempting but needs care. The single-cosine model linearizes to ordinary least squares, but asymmetric rhythms "require additional harmonics" ([MetricGate cosinor docs](https://metricgate.com/docs/cosinor-rhythmometry/)). A one- or two-harmonic fit flattens exactly the post-lunch dip the planner relies on. Use cosinor only to report a single "your peak hour" summary, and use the kernel-smoothed posterior for scheduling. Report confidence in categories (fewer than 7 days "typical curve", 7–20 "learning", 21 or more "personalized") rather than as numbers.

## A greedy plan refined by local search fits a day into milliseconds

Commercial schedulers show the baseline. Motion places tasks by duration, earliest start, deadline and priority, splits long tasks into chunks, respects working hours and breaks, and warns when tasks don't fit or deadlines are at risk ([Motion](https://www.usemotion.com/help/time-management/auto-scheduling/reference-auto-scheduling)). Reclaim weighs priority against due date, orders tasks of the same priority by due date, and lets higher priorities overbook lower ones ([Reclaim](https://help.reclaim.ai/en/articles/6207587-how-reclaim-manages-your-schedule-automatically)). Sunsama fits tasks around events and pushes leftovers into "judgement calls" the user can keep, move or discard ([Sunsama](https://help.sunsama.com/docs/usage-guides/daily-planning)). None of them publicly documents an energy-curve term in placement, so energy matching is a real differentiator for an on-device planner.

Priority should combine value per unit time with deadline pressure. SAFe's Weighted Shortest Job First divides cost of delay by job duration, where cost of delay is business value plus time criticality plus risk reduction, scored on a relative Fibonacci scale ([SAFe WSJF](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20161225031923mp_/http://www.scaledagileframework.com/wsjf)). For a personal planner that becomes CoD = importance + urgency(t) + an unblocking bonus, divided by the bias-corrected duration. No empirical study validates any particular urgency curve. Either an exponential with τ = 48 hours or a hyperbolic 1/(1 + slack/8h) is defensible, and both should be exposed as tunable. Hard deadlines today should pin a task to the earliest feasible slot regardless of score. The per-slot score then adds energy fit. Expected demand is d = 0.2·P(Light) + 0.5·P(Medium) + 0.9·P(Heavy), so classifier uncertainty flows straight into scheduling. The match term penalizes under-powered placements (heavy task, low-energy slot) three times as hard as over-powered ones. Multiplying the energy term by d makes it matter mainly for heavy work, which mirrors the finding that simple tasks barely vary by time of day. The full score is a weighted sum: urgency 1.0, importance 1.0, energy 1.2, quick-win bonus 0.3, context-switch penalty 0.6, lateness penalty 2.0. All are unvalidated defaults that the feedback learners adjust.

For 30 or fewer tasks, the build sequence has five steps. First, merge light same-category tasks into one batch of 60 minutes or less, which makes switch-cost reduction structural. Second, check capacity and move overflow to tomorrow, lowest score first and never anything due today. Third, construct greedily, letting heavy tasks claim the best-energy windows. Fourth, refine with about 3,000 iterations of seeded pairwise-swap or insertion local search on a single objective, which runs in roughly 1–3 ms. The fixed random seed keeps the plan stable on reload. Fifth, lay the plan out with breaks. The Hungarian algorithm gives an exact optimum if the day is cut into equal slots, but it cannot express switching costs or variable durations, so it is a fallback rather than the main engine.

One coherence point matters for constants. The research supports both per-task duration inflation and day-level slack, and stacking both naively over-pads the day. The recommended combination is to lay out each task at its **learned median** duration and fill the day to at most **75% of free time**. Spread the slack as roughly 10-minute buffers after each 60–90-minute block, so overruns don't cascade. Then decide overflow using the **Monte Carlo P80 of the day's total**. Add a 10–15-minute break after 90 minutes or more of contiguous focus and a 5-minute buffer between heavy tasks. Enforce the 4-hour heavy cap and at most two heavy blocks back-to-back. These numbers inherit the weak evidence described above. The 75% fill ratio in particular is a practitioner convention inferred from planning-fallacy data, not a measured optimum.

| Plan default | Value | Evidence strength |
|---|---|---|
| Focus block / break | 60 min (25–90) / ~20% of block, 5–20 min | Weak (DeskTime observational, BRAC contested); breaks restore vigor (moderate) |
| Heavy work cap / target | 240 / 180 min per day | Moderate-weak (Ericsson deliberate-practice review) |
| Schedule fill | ≤ 75% of free time, distributed buffers | Inference from planning-fallacy data |
| Cold-start duration multiplier | 1.25 (≤ 30 min) → 1.5 (≥ 120 min) | Strong for direction; short-task value assumed |
| Warm-up quick win | One task ≤ 15 min first | Moderate (progress principle) |
| Heavy-task placement | First peak block; energy weight × demand | Moderate (synchrony effect) |
| Willpower-depletion model | Off | Failed replication |

## Feedback learners and one-line reasons keep the plan honest

Two lightweight learners can adapt the planner without heavy machinery. The first is a **Beta-Bernoulli Thompson sampler over three or four strategy "arms"**: energy-matched, hardest-first, quick-wins and deadline-first, each implemented as a preset rescaling of the score weights. Each day, sample from each arm's Beta posterior, play the best, and update with α += r, β += 1 − r ([Russo et al., Tutorial on Thompson Sampling](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1707.02038)). The reward can be fractional, for example half the fraction of planned tasks completed plus half the agreement between planned and actual order. Habits drift, so discount the posteriors toward the prior each day. Russo et al. describe exactly this nonstationary variant, which pulls parameters back toward prior values. A rate of about 0.05 per day toward Beta(2,2) gives a memory of roughly 20 decisions. Start the energy-matched arm at Beta(3,2) to encode the synchrony evidence. Explore only once per day at plan time, and say so ("Trying a quick-wins order today"), so exploration never feels random.

The second learner is a **pairwise perceptron on the score weights**, adapted from Joachims' "click > skip-above" rule. That rule treats implicit feedback as relative judgments because clicks are biased toward top positions ([The Morning Paper on Joachims 2002](https://blog.acolyer.org/2015/08/27/optimizing-search-engines-using-clickthrough-data/)). Dragging task i above tasks j₁…j_m yields preferences i ≻ j_k at full weight. Completing i before a task j that was planned earlier yields i ≻ j at half weight. Deferring a task yields "everything completed" ≻ deferred at half weight. Silent acceptance yields no pairs, to avoid reinforcing position bias. When the weighted feature difference falls below a margin of 0.1, step the weights by η = 0.05. Then clip each weight to [0.05, 3× default] so importance and urgency can never flip sign and explanations stay meaningful. Completion order is confounded by the plan's own ordering. Without the small amount of randomization the bandit supplies, this learner can simply ratify whatever order it proposed, so keep its completion-derived updates at reduced weight.

Explanations should be partial, specific and attached to a control. Google's PAIR guidebook advises that "the best explanation is likely a partial one", with more detail on request. It recommends explaining data sources and gaps, notes that "numeric confidence indicators are risky", and says that setting the expectation the system will learn as users teach it helps build trust ([Google PAIR, Explainability + Trust](https://pair.withgoogle.com/chapter/People%20+%20AI%20Guidebook%20-%20Explainability%20+%20Trust.pdf)). Vendor practice is consistent. Rise ties each named energy zone to a recommended action ([Rise](https://risescience.com/blog/how-to-have-more-energy-in-the-morning)). Sunsama's planner gives a briefing on the chosen order and makes every action undoable ([Sunsama](https://help.sunsama.com/docs/usage-guides/daily-planning)). The practical rule is to compute each score term's contribution for the chosen task-slot pair, show the largest as a single sentence, and add a second clause only if it reaches 50% of the first. Examples: "Heavy work → your 10–11 AM peak (based on 3 weeks of your focus data)". "Light task in your 2 PM dip — saves your peak for deep work". "Grouped with 3 other email tasks to cut context switches". "Moved to tomorrow to keep heavy work under 4 h". During cold start, use honest phrasing such as "late morning, when most people focus best; we'll learn your hours." The model internals provide these explanations almost for free. For effort, show the top three per-token log-likelihood-ratio contributions ("'design' → Heavy"). For duration, show multiplicative factors exp(x_j·μ_j) ("Thesis project ×1.4"). Personal evidence such as "you finished 70% of heavy tasks started 9–11" is the most persuasive form. Every line should come with Pin, Move, Not today and "Don't schedule heavy work at this time" controls, because those taps are the training signal for both learners.

The pitfalls cluster into four traps. **Self-confirming loops** are the most insidious. Accepted suggestions trained as labels, model-suggested estimates trained as user bias, and completion order trained as preference all teach the model to agree with itself. The fixes are provenance tracking, low weight on acceptances and no-update-on-acceptance rules. **Anchoring** is a related risk: showing a P80 may stretch work to fill it, so monitor whether residuals on accepted suggestions drift upward. **Garbage actuals** from forgotten timers or instant check-offs need winsorizing and exclusion rules. **Drift and windup** need count-based rather than wall-clock forgetting, so a two-week vacation doesn't erase the model, plus covariance caps. Finally, everything persists as a few hundred kilobytes of JSON with floats rounded to three decimals. All signals stay on the device, which the settings page should say plainly along with a one-tap reset.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the problem. The science does not supply a correct schedule, only a handful of robust directional facts. People underestimate durations multiplicatively. Demanding work is somewhat better at a personal peak that sits a few hours apart for larks and owls. Breaks restore vigor. Heavy cognitive work saturates around four hours. Switching and unfinished tasks are costly. Almost every specific number beyond those facts, from 52/17 to the urgency curve's time constant, is an engineering guess. That makes conjugate Bayesian machinery the natural fit rather than an over-engineered one. Pseudo-counts and prior means are an explicit, inspectable way to say "start with the literature's best guess, and let roughly five to fifteen of this user's own observations outvote it." Every learned quantity also stays decomposable into a sentence the user can read and correct.

The decisive engineering choices are therefore about evidence flow, not model capacity. Which events count as labels, at what weight, from which provenance, and with what forgetting matter more than whether the classifier is naive Bayes or logistic regression. The constants here should ship as tunables with prequential metrics logged locally: classifier log loss, duration mean absolute log error, P80 coverage near 80%, and plan-completion rate per strategy arm. Then the planner's first months of use become the validation study the literature never ran for single-user daily planning.
